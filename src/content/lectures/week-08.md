## Outline

- self-checkout doesn't remove labour, it relocates it onto the shopper,
  unpaid — the interface's whole job is to make that relocation feel like
  a convenience
- weight sensors, "unexpected item" prompts and camera overlays as loss
  prevention wearing a helpfulness costume
- who actually captures the wage savings, and how far that is from the
  shopper doing the extra work

## The argument

Self-checkout doesn't remove the labour of scanning and bagging
groceries — it relocates it onto the shopper, unpaid, and dresses the
relocation up as a convenience the shopper asked for. The interface's
cheerful tone ("please place item in bagging area") is doing real work
here: it frames a cost shift as a courtesy.

The weight sensors and "unexpected item" prompts that interrupt you
aren't mainly there to help you shop faster — most exist to catch theft,
and they treat every shopper as a suspect to make that catch statistically
worthwhile. The wage savings the store books from cutting checkout staff
don't return to the shopper doing the extra work; they're captured
entirely by the operator, and the shopper's only compensation is the time
a human cashier would have saved them.

**Question to carry:** if self-checkout is a convenience, why does it need
cameras pointed at the person supposedly being helped?
