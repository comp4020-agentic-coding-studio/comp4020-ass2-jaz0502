## Outline

- "the machine can't make exceptions" as a sentence that only became
  available once a machine, not a person, was making the decision
- default paths built around an imagined typical user, and what happens to
  everyone the gate didn't imagine
- accessible alternatives that exist on paper but not in practice: distance,
  visibility, staffing

## The argument

"The machine can't make exceptions" is a sentence that only became sayable
once a machine, rather than a person, was the one enforcing the rule. A
staffed gate could always weigh a specific situation; a fare gate can only
recognise the cases its designers anticipated, and everyone outside that
anticipated shape becomes, by definition, an exception the system wasn't
built to handle.

The accessible alternative usually exists somewhere in the station's
paperwork — a wider gate, an intercom, a staffed counter — but its
distance from the default path, in metres and in minutes, is itself a
design decision. A gate that's technically accessible but three platforms
away from where everyone else enters isn't really offering an
alternative; it's offering a technicality that satisfies an audit without
satisfying the person standing at the machine.

**Question to carry:** whose body and situation did the "default" path get
built around, and who decided that was reasonable?
