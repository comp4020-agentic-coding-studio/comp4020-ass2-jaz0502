A toll gate doesn't fail the way a jammed ticket machine fails — it
succeeds exactly as designed, and the failure is that you can't find out
the price until the bill arrives. That's not a UI bug. It's a concession
contract wearing a transponder.

## Outline

- e-tolling as the clearest case this semester of a business model, not a
  usability failure, showing up as a machine: the price genuinely can't be
  known until after you've already paid it
- reading a concession contract to find the incentive a toll gate's
  interface was built to protect — a minimum-revenue guarantee behaves very
  differently from a straight per-kilometre fee, and the gate can't tell you
  which one it's enforcing
- what "dynamic pricing" is doing when the driver has no way to see it
  coming

## The argument

Most machines in this course fail in a way you can point to: a button
that doesn't respond, an instruction that doesn't match what happens
next. A toll gate is different — it does exactly what it was built to do,
and what it was built to do is protect a concession contract's revenue
guarantee, not tell you the price before you owe it. There's no bug to
find, because nothing is broken.

That's what makes it the clearest business-model-as-interface case this
semester studies: a minimum-revenue guarantee and a straight
per-kilometre fee produce very different incentives for the operator, and
the gate gives you no way to tell which one is actually governing your
trip. "Dynamic pricing" sounds like a feature. From the driver's seat,
it's a bill you can't predict, arriving after the decision it should have
informed.

> **Question to carry:** if you can't find out the price until after you've
> paid it, in what sense did you agree to it?
