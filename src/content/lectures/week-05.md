## Outline

- a kiosk doesn't just automate a check-in desk, it relocates who is
  answerable when something goes wrong
- "the system won't let me" as a sentence a machine makes possible that a
  person at a desk couldn't get away with
- where the human escalation path is hidden, and why that's rarely an
  accident of layout

## The argument

"The system won't let me" is a sentence a kiosk makes possible in a way a
person at a desk rarely could. A human employee who refused you for an
opaque reason would be expected to explain it, or find someone who could —
a machine gets to simply stop, and the refusal arrives with no author
attached. That's not a side effect of automation; it's one of the things
automation is for.

Relocating the decision from a person to a machine also relocates the
liability. Nobody at the airline is wrong when a kiosk fails, because the
kiosk didn't decide anything — it executed rules somebody else wrote, and
that somebody is now several layers removed from the passenger standing at
the gate. The escalation path still exists; it's just rarely where the
kiosk points first, and that placement is a choice.

**Question to carry:** when a machine tells you "no" with no explanation,
who used to have to give you one?
