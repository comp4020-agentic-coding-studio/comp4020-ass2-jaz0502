## Outline

- the captive user at their most vulnerable: unwell, in pain, or supporting
  someone who is, with no realistic option to walk away
- what a check-in terminal assumes about literacy, language and composure —
  and what it costs a person who doesn't match the assumption
- efficiency gains for the institution against the burden shifted onto the
  person least able to carry it

## The argument

Every machine this course studies asks something of a captive user, but a
hospital check-in kiosk asks it of someone who may be in pain, frightened,
or speaking for a person who is. It assumes a level of literacy, language
fluency and composure that is a reasonable average across all patients and
a real failure for a specific one, at the exact moment that patient has
the least capacity to push back or ask for help.

The efficiency the kiosk buys the institution — fewer staff at the desk,
faster throughput — is real, but it's paid for by whoever doesn't match
the assumed default, and that cost never shows up on any dashboard the
hospital actually looks at. A form that's merely inconvenient for most
people can be genuinely unsafe for the person it wasn't built with in
mind.

**Question to carry:** if a kiosk were designed against the worst day
someone could be having, rather than an average one, what would it ask
first instead?
